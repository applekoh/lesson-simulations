How air-conditioning cools a room

An air conditioner doesn't create cold — it moves heat out of the room and releases it outside. Think of it like a water pump: a pump can push water uphill, against the direction it would naturally flow, and in the same way an air conditioner "pumps" heat from the cooler room to the warmer outdoors — the opposite of the direction heat moves on its own. Forcing heat the "wrong" way takes energy, which is why the unit has to be powered. Four components and one circulating refrigerant do the job. Click any of them to see how.

Full loop

This is a split system: an indoor unit (evaporator + expansion valve) and an outdoor unit (compressor + condenser), joined by two pipes through the wall. One continuous loop of refrigerant runs through it all, and its colour shows what state the refrigerant is in: blue = cold refrigerant (a low-pressure liquid–gas mix that boils into cold gas), red = hot high-pressure gas (straight after the compressor), amber = warm high-pressure liquid (after it has condensed).

Each unit is a heat exchanger. The indoor unit draws in warm air from the room, passes it over the cold coil to take heat out of it, and blows cool air back into the room. The outdoor unit does the opposite: it draws in ambient outdoor air, passes it over the hot coil to dump heat into it, and blows warm air outside. The outdoor unit actually releases more heat than the room lost, because it must also get rid of the energy the compressor used.

The refrigerant is a fixed mass going round a closed loop, yet each component is an open system with flow through it — that is the open-vs-closed distinction in one picture.

Tap any component to zoom in and watch how it works — the coils changing phase, the compressor squeezing, the valve throttling.

Nice work

A teaching model, not to scale. The refrigerant is drawn as a single loop of tubing; real systems add a receiver, filter-drier and other parts. The expansion valve is shown indoors at the evaporator inlet, so both pipes crossing the wall stay single-phase (warm liquid in, cold gas out).
