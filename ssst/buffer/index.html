Inside a Buffer

A buffer is a mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base sitting together in water. Add a little strong acid or strong base and the mixture absorbs it, so the pH barely moves — until the mixture runs out. Add a dose and watch what happens to the molecules, the amounts, and the pH.

HA + H₂O ⇌ A− + H₃O+

This particular weak acid has a pKa of 4.74. The buffer starts with equal amounts of HA and A−, so the [HA]/[A−] ratio is 1, and log₁₀(1) = 0 — which is why the fresh buffer sits at pH = pKa = 4.74 before anything is added.

The solution

Strong base
HA — weak acid A− — conjugate base H+ — proton

HA is A− with a proton (H+) attached — so a conversion is simply a proton coming or going.

When we add a small amount of strong acid, it reacts with A− from the buffer to form more HA: [A−] goes down by that small amount and [HA] goes up by the same amount, so the [HA]/[A−] ratio increases. The [H₃O+] in the solution increases very slightly and the pH decreases very slightly.

When we add a small amount of strong base, the OH− ions react with HA from the buffer to form more A−: this time the [HA]/[A−] ratio decreases, which decreases [H₃O+] very slightly and the pH increases very slightly.
